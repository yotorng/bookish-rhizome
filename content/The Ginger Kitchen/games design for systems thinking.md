# Systems
Understanding systems thinking as an attempt to understand parts in relation to the whole, of recognising **interconnections between the many parts of a system**
- Patterns

Complex is not the same as complicated
- Complicated → many parts, but size and behaviour of each part does not change ; The way the system behaves is linear and predictable
- Complex → different parts, interacting entities, each part of the system responds in different ways as conditions change, whole system is modified over time
	- Emergent properties
	- Constant change over time → have to engage with change upfront
	- Make decisions without all the information we'd like, and still be responsible for the consequences
	- Have to cope with inevitable side effects

Value of understanding systems:
- [[#Threshold Models|Thresholds/Tipping Points]]— Social-ecological systems can exist in different states. When a system changes too much, it crosses a threshold and it will shift to a new state, often quite abruptly.
- [[#Feedback Loops]] — When systems react to their changes, that’s a feedback loop. Positive feedback loops are powerful drivers of change.
- [[#Prisoner's Dilemma]] — Situations where, if everyone would like to do something but, if everyone does it, everyone is worse off, are known as ‘prisoner’s dilemmas’. We see these situations emerge again and again in very different contexts.
- **[[Emergent Strategy|Emergence]]** — When vast numbers of components interact in a system with no central control or leader, they display complex collective behaviour that is impossible to predict by looking at the individual components.
# Models
Modelling ≠ understanding
- All creatures extract patterns and regularity, that is to say, all living beings act in relation to models
- Experimental models that are qualitative rather than quantitative, and rarely rely on software simulations. They are intended to **illuminate aspects of a system**, to help people think through complex problems, and to provide a space for conversation and reflection.
- Models are inherently simplified representations of a system, which begs the following qns: **What kinds of models are then useful? What should we choose to adopt?**

 Details ≠ Utility
- Creating a distilled, stripped back version of a model can be **useful**, especially in contexts of informing what kind of choices and decisions we need to make
	- This process is called abstraction
	- Choosing what details to include/exclude ultimately rests on your **intended outcomes → what specifically do you want to achieve?**
	- E.g. Google Earth vs Mountain hiking route map → You don’t need the most textured or comprehensive map showcasing all the specific satellite imagery to hike a mountain

# Why use games? Thinking through games as…

## Interactive, imperfect models of complex systems **_(systems pov)_**
- Function as an explicit model, help us translate and share our different (mental) models of the systems we inhabit
	- Turning our implicit understandings and assumptions into explicit actions and consequences
- Games can help to connect different component parts of a system, including its feedback loops and thresholds
	- Opportunity to unlearn linear thinking where A causes B → games can model systems' complexity and interconnectedness
- Interactivity of a game allows different actors to **experiment with hypothetical interventions and understand system properties**

## An experimental container **_(people pov)_**
- Invite people to inhabit different perspectives, perhaps unlearn assumptions, and intervene in a problem/system as active players
	- e.g. working selfishly or selflessly within a system
- Reframe the notion of playing to **win**
	- What does **winning** mean?

## A catalyst for **collective intelligence _(emergence pov)_**
- Games fundamentally allow for players' **agency** as well as **emergence**, while also letting players surrender their illusion of control
	- Good representation of systems in games showcase their unpredictable elements, incorporating randomness, contingencies and uncertainties. Players have to adapt to changing circumstances and unexpected events
- Not about designing the best game, but rather how do you design for people to **think better together**
- Games as an encounter & vehicle, a center of gravity for convening people around a shared issue

# How to design a game based on systems thinking?

## Step 1: Mapping a system

- Not about categorising your system precisely, but instead: adopting this tool to the extent that it is **useful**, depending on what kinds of judgement you want to make
- Activity can be iterative, generative, useful to create a sprawling overview

Dimensions to include:
- Physical map (immediate)
- Physical map (surroundings)
- Primary Users
	- Value of thinking beyond human actors, can consider non-human roles too
- Secondary Users
- Values
	- Thinking of values as things that you might want to retain in a system
	- Recognise that there might be trade-offs within values that a system might espouse (e.g. in an sg school, perhaps an instance would be a balancing act between discipline and encouraging freedom/creativity to explore amongst students)
- Issues
	- Values & issues as mirror reflections of each other. Typically issues that a system faces tend to reveal its very own values and principles
	- What is at stake? What is being stressed?
- Rules (can be both tacit or explicit)
	- Trade-offs can also happen between rules
- Shocks
	- Can happen on different scales
	- Not all shocks are negative 
	- **Characteristic shocks:** Regular, expected, system is familiar with and knows how to handle or absorb its impact
	- **Large, infrequent shocks:** System might not be able to carry on as normal
	- **Unknown shocks:** System unable to anticipate, and might fundamentally be not the same
- Timeline - you can decide on the scale of this
	- Daily
	- Annual
	- History

## Step 2: Movement & tensions within the system: Shocks, feedback loops, tipping points and prisoners' dilemmas

### Threshold Models:
Identifying how shocks travel through a system, and how shocks can ripple and trigger for even more other shocks within a system.

Scale: Small (can include different subsystems that is affected), medium, large
- Decide what this means to you and your use-case

Domains:
- **Biophysical**: Infrastructure, flow of materials in a physical setting or environment
- **Economic**: Exchange of money, information and goods within systems
- **Social**: Governs interactions between groups of users/stakeholders

![[Pasted image 20260901123448.png]]
_e.g. something small-scale happens in the biophysical realm of a system (e.g. a tree falls along the edge of a forest, blocking a road), that initial 'shock' can lead to a medium-scale ripple-effect in the economic domain (e.g. the road is a key delivery path for goods, and trucks are held up because of this tree - a supply chain 'shock'), which might result in another ripple effect that triggers more other shocks within the system._  → effective in demonstrating how shocks can travel through a system

Each movement can be thought of as a **tipping point** where the change in the system _crosses a threshold, and results in a new change in the system_, where the shock has a sizeable impact that pushes the system to the a different state (moving the system from basin A to basin B)
![[Pasted image 20260901123509.png]]

Resilience as the capacity of a system to absorb shock - e.g. where the bigger/deeper basin of a system can technically 'absorb' shock even more than the shallow basin of B.

### Feedback Loops:

See the green cycle in the threshold model below → where an initial change results in the system reacting to their change in a cyclical fashion, eventually _returning back_ to itself ; in other words, where the output affects the input eventually
![[Pasted image 20260901123522.png]]

**Positive feedback loops:** Spiral and accelerates the shock the more it continues, pulling the system away from equilibrium
![[Pasted image 20260901123537.png]]

**Negative feedback loops:** Shocks that try to dampen the initial change, attempts to bring the system back to equilibrium

### Prisoner's Dilemma:
Agents typically try to get the best outcome for themselves. Prisoners dilemma are cases where the best scenario is when you are selfish, but if everyone is selfish, everyone is worse-off. Very prevalent in human scenarios and systems.

Shadow of the future
- An additional extension of the prisoner's dilemma, accounting for how people interact over multiple interactions over time
- If you interact more than 1 time, or if you know the interaction is coming to an end, you will be more likely to 'betray' the person
## Step 3: Game Ideation
game design approach + system properties
### Appending **resources & rules**: feedback loops
- Represent the system through resources, and think about conditions that cause the resource to decrease/recover/increase (aka rules)
	- A field is represented by 5 tokens of grass.
	- When a football game happens, 3 grass tokens gets removed (the grass dies). When there is a rest period, 1 grass token gets added back (grass can grow)
	- Feedback loop (**when the resource itself affects the activity/intervention**): Every day, roll a 10 sided die. If the die roll is equal to or less than the amount of grass, there is a game. Otherwise, there is no football game.
	- ![[Pasted image 20260901123646.png]]
- Starting questions:
	- What is the resource and activity to model?
	- What happens to the resource every time the activity takes place?
	- What influences the frequency of the activity? How do the resources factor in?
	- Useful to also play with what kinds of **breaks** can stop the feedback loop, interventions

### **Modding/hacking games**: Tipping points/thresholds

You may not need a bespoke design! Hacking and modding games can also alleviate (game designing) resource issues, and serve as useful starting points
- Useful to think about **blackjack** as an application of tipping points
	- Player's hand: Resource accumulation over time
	- Add card: Adding pressure/risk to a system
	- Stop drawing cards: Managing the risk that a system can hold, steadying it
	- Bust: Tipping point of the system
		- 21 points is the formal tipping point, but players organise themselves around the knowledge of this and how much risk they can 'hold' (e.g. I better stop drawing cards when I reach 15/16 points)
		- At what point did the system feel 'fragile' and seemed like it was nearing its tipping point?
		- Informal thresholds exist and are real for players
- Think about:
	- What does the win outcome of not reaching tipping point represent (e.g. climate overshoot)
	- What do the actions mean (mitigating risk when choosing actions to take)
	- What do the roles represent (players/dealer/players who overshoot)
	- What do the game pieces represent (what would a card mean?)
		- BY extension - what does the uncertainty of the cards mean (what does the uncertainty model)
- Bridging actions and meaning
- Modifying can look like:
	- Tweak goals, rules, etc etc
	- E.g. instead of individual overshoot, what about implementing a collective threshold of 80 points?

### **Competition, collaboration, uncertainty & payoff**: Prisoners dilemma

- Recognising players as **agents,** think deeply about what your players' behaviours would be organising around, and **why.**
- Can go through iterations of prisoners' dilemma, varying players, pay-offs, and/or playing the scenario multiple times
- Games can also function as multiplayer prisoners dilemma - e.g. during evac bingo where you play as families, with limited resources, prioritising their own survival instead of collaborating and consolidating resources together
- Elements to think about when designing games:
	- Balance uncertainty & pay-off → how scores are shaped
	- Trust and communication across players are **designable surfaces**
		- Can players speak with each other?
		- Do you want to create competition or collaboration? Make sure that this choice is also an intentional one.

### Starting Points
Choose either a feedback loop, tipping point or a prisoner's dilemma that you've identified in the system
- When ____ increases, _____changes because ____.
- (Feedback loop) The system creates a loop because ____ affects ____,and then ____ affects ____ in return.
- (Tipping point) The system reached a tipping point when ____ crosses a certain threshold, causing ____ to shift into a different state
- (Prisoner's Dilemma) The system creates a dilemma because each actor benefits from ____individually, but when everyone does so, ____ becomes worse for everyone.

Other starting points (from the system mapping exercise):
- What value do you want to preserve in the system?
- Is there an issue to tackle? What is at stake?
- Is there an opportunity for transformation or shocks?
- Is the system on the verge of a tipping point?
- What kinds of trade-offs exist? (e.g. if I do XXX, I would not be able to achieve YYY.)
	- Busy Mayors game set during the first warning of an impending typhoon and when the disaster hits your town
		- Trade-off between information and the ability to act. In a climate disaster, the more information you accumulate, the longer you wait, the harder it is for you to comprehensively respond at times (e.g. hurricane & evacuation)
		- Trade-off between climate mitigation and political power (and the false binary)

# Additional considerations when designing games!

## Design Mechanics & Levers

### The role of meaningful actions: agency & uncertainty

Players' agency to choose actions begets **uncertainty.** Games inherently function as **uncertainty machines**, allowing for unique dynamics to also emerge
- What kind of uncertainty are you trying to introduce? What is the function of uncertainty, what can it reveal about the system, how might you use it?
	- e.g. blackjack & the uncertainty of each card each time you draw. Players are **making a choice** about how much risk they can 'take'
- Game design considerations: How do you shape agency in a game? Designers can restrict or empower agency.
- Knowing your target player audience is important.

### Tactical weirdness/Distancing

- Useful for reframing, allows players to reperceive the issue without drawing on their existing mental models
- Good for heavy, traumatic issues, but trade-off might be in terms of accuracy of representation
	- E.g. game about bullying, but harnessing the metaphor of ghosts
- Sometimes you need the proxy model, but **understand what the implications are**. This is also where debriefing, discussion and facilitation is incredibly valuable

### Replayability

Depending on your use case, players might play it 1x or multiple times. When designing for 1x play, think about how you want to guide people to hit certain landmarks or decisions. You don’t need your game to yield 100 possibilities in some cases.

### Representation & fidelity of models

Sometimes it's more useful to represent the dynamics and tensions of a system instead of representing its mechanisms in a neat, technocratic fashion. E.g. if you want to design a game about teacher burn out or inequality in SG, maybe the point shouldn’t be to just replicate the system for the sake of representing it 'well'. The best games need not replicate a well-worn truth. Instead, it might be worth examining what exactly a game can achieve (see above section on [[#Why use games? Thinking through games as…]])

- Think deeply about what a game can **achieve**, your mechanics and what they represent
- Finding the correct scale of intervention is important
	- Systemic/individual scales for different reasons
- Depth: Weigh the trade-offs between how realistic & detailed, vs depth
- Recognise the disjunct between how you behave in a game vs in real life. Models skew behaviour!!!!
- To control is to distort…All models are wrong but some are useful…!!!
	- Wavicle (Observing light as a particle vs a wave [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observer_effect_(physics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observer_effect_\(physics\))) duality

### Procedural rhetoric: Win conditions, rules, scoring and valuation

What does winning mean? What does it **represent**?
Understand how models skew behaviour
The rules…which then begs the question of what is **meaningful play**
Marrying your quantitative values(i.e. scoring) and values(i.e. principles)
- Scoring is seductive, but it can also mask
- Valuation is inherently an imaginative act → helps to also account for variance in people's perceptions

### On the value of simplicity:

David Finnigan's games on climate resilience and adaptation → deliberately simple but surface difficult issues of trade-offs to make in climate emergency (e.g. which family gets to evacuate in times of crisis) ; while at the same time loosening tense emotions through games
- Abstraction & elegance can be incredibly powerful.

### Mechanics & concepts
**Adjacency:** Distance between game elements, useful in mapping relationships between resources/players
**Balance:** Making a game playable…balancing between difficulty, fun, and fairness (there can be some math involved when you think about scoring)

## Process

### Do you really need a game? Sometimes no…but also sometimes you might even need *multiple* games.

Be clear about what strategic outcomes you really want to achieve, and how other workshops, programmes, and other modalities might not be able to afford. Why does this specific case warrant these extensive scenarios, emergent behaviour, etc etc? (see above section for [[#Why use games? Thinking through games as…]])

At times, modular games can function as small-scale interventions for specific contexts. There is value in drilling down and distilling into the smallest possible units.
- Different games to move around different shocks of a system
- E.g. Busy mayors vs evac bingo offers 2 different pov of the same overarching issue of disaster mitigation and response. Reveals how interventions in  system does not have be a full, totalising game. Instead, reveals many different actors interact differently in a system.

### Situating games

Consider the before, during and after of your game. Be intentional about what you want to accomplish at every stage. Framing the game at the start is also equally important. Decide how much information you would need to reveal, and what aspects of the game you would want to let emerge amongst players organically.

### Playtesting games

- Iteration!!
- Digging deeper when players feedback. (e.g. "game was too long" doesn't mean the actionable is to shorten it, but instead implies that they were disengaged at some point. Figure out where + when, and then sharpen it.)

### On debriefing & facilitation

Debriefing is as important as the gameplay itself. Set up space to calibrate how much of 'self' to bring into the game.
Facilitation can be a multistakeholder process.
E.g. David Finnigan's climate games, he facilitates the gameplay, but let the subject matter experts(scientists) debrief

**Sample debrief template for games:**
1. **What happened?**

| Dimension     | Guiding questions                                           |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Resource      | What does the model track?                                  |
| Player action | What can players do that changes the resource?              |
| Rules         | How do actions affect the system and players' interactions? |
| Consequence   | What happens as the system changes?                         |

2. **Why did it happen?**
- How much of the system was revealed in the beginning, what was hidden?
- What could have gone differently, due to player choices or random chance?
- What trade-offs exist within the game?

3. **So what?**
- How did the game represent the system it was trying to model? What did the game miss out on?
- Where might this show opportunities for intervention (in your work or otherwise)? What next?

### Evaluating games

Typically qualitative feedback. Tie it back to whether the game was able to induce the type of conversations that the designer was trying to achieve

Dimensions can include: change in meaning, connection and power. See [CreaTures framework](https://creaturesframework.org/funding/creatures-dimensions.html) for more inspiration.

Think about what kind of scales are useful for your stated goals. Scale in:
- **distance** (scaling out in terms of reach, but also replication and duplication), 
- **depth** (scaling deep can imply change in relationships, cultural values and beliefs), and 
- **direction** (scaling up can mean changing institutions at the level of policy, rules and laws)
